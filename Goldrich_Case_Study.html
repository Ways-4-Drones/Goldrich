The Lawrence J. Goldrich Institute for NeuroHealth

The Lawrence J. Goldrich Institute for Integrated NeuroHealth opened to patients in November 2022 as part of the Macon & Joan Brock Virginia Health Sciences at Old Dominion University. It was made possible by a $15 million gift from Virginia Beach residents Larry and Janice Goldrich. Larry Goldrich lived with Parkinson’s disease, and the gift reflects his belief that a specialized neurohealth program could meaningfully improve the lives of patients throughout Hampton Roads and the surrounding region. The Institute is the first comprehensive neurohealth center of its kind in the Hampton Roads region. Before it opened, patients in this area who needed specialized neurodegenerative care often had to travel outside Virginia entirely.

The care model is explicitly multidisciplinary. The Institute’s team includes physicians, physician assistants (PAs), nurse practitioners (NPs), nurses, occupational therapists, psychologists, social workers, and patient navigators. Patients and their families do not just see a single doctor — they are supported by a coordinated team across the full arc of their care.

Clinic Operations

The Comprehensive Memory Center (CMC) currently operates at a provider utilization rate of approximately 60%. In other words, four out of every ten available appointment slots go unfilled in a given week. The empty slots do not reflect a lack of demand: the clinic has an ever-growing waiting list. Instead, the problem is that the scheduling process does not reliably match available capacity to patient demand. Improving the schedule, therefore, means the CMC could accommodate more patients at its current capacity.

The Institute’s leadership has asked for an evidence-based review of current scheduling policies and a set of specific recommendations for improvement. By suggesting improvements to the schedule, our goal is to better deploy the providers and resources at the CMC, and ultimately improve patient access to care.

Understanding Patients

Strome College of Business  |  Old Dominion University  |  Macon & Joan Brock Virginia Health Sciences

The Comprehensive Memory Clinic (CMC) treats patients with dementia; the following sections describe the clinical and operational challenges unique to dementia patients.

What Is Dementia?

Dementia is not a single disease. It is an umbrella term for a group of conditions characterized by progressive decline in memory, reasoning, language, and the ability to carry out everyday activities. It results from damage to brain cells that impairs their ability to communicate with one another. As the damage spreads, symptoms worsen over time.

Approximately 70% of dementia cases are caused by Alzheimer’s disease. Other common forms include vascular dementia (caused by reduced blood flow to the brain), Lewy body dementia, and frontotemporal dementia. Each has a somewhat different clinical presentation, but all are progressive and, currently, irreversible. There is no cure for any form of dementia.

According to the Virginia Department of Health, approximately 164,000 Virginians are affected by Alzheimer’s disease, and that number is projected to nearly double by 2050 as the baby boomer generation ages. Dementia is among the leading causes of death and disability in the United States, and it is widely believed to be underdiagnosed. Many people living with early-stage cognitive decline have never been evaluated by a specialist.

The Importance of Early Detection

While dementia cannot be reversed, early detection matters enormously. Diagnosis at an early stage can improve quality of life, family planning, and financial and legal preparation. Moreover, some treatments are only available at specific points in the disease’s progression. For patients diagnosed at later stages, the clinical focus shifts to symptom management and to supporting caregivers so that patients can remain in their homes and communities as long as possible.

This is one of the reasons why scheduling access matters so much at the Goldrich Institute. Faster access to the clinic can determine the treatments for which a patient is eligible.

The Diagnosis Journey

Diagnosing dementia is not a single appointment. It unfolds across a structured process that typically spans several weeks and involves multiple members of the care team.

1. Initial Evaluation

A new patient’s first appointment is typically conducted by a nurse practitioner, physician assistant, or nurse. This visit involves cognitive and memory testing, a detailed patient history, and an interview with the patient’s caregiver. Additional testing, including brain imaging and laboratory work, is often ordered at this stage.

2. Physician Consultation & Formal Diagnosis

After all diagnostic data has been collected and reviewed, a physician makes the formal clinical determination. Physician time is protected for this step: the Institute’s care model is designed so that physicians work at the top of their clinical licensure, spending their time on the cases that require physician-level expertise.

3. Treatment Planning & Ongoing Care

Once a diagnosis is made, the care team develops a treatment plan, which may include infusion therapy, education, caregiver support, medication management, and annual follow-up appointments.

The Central Role of Caregivers

Patients’ caregivers are a foundation of the CMC’s treatment plans. Providers rely on caregivers to notice changes in behavior and cognition, to manage medications and appointments, and to advocate for the patient if the patient is unable to do so. Without caregivers, providers may have an incomplete picture of the patient’s history and medical condition.

This creates an important operational reality for the case: if the caregiver cannot attend an appointment, the patient may be unable to attend as well. Caregiver conflicts — a family member who is ill, has a work obligation, or simply cannot arrange transportation — are one of the primary drivers of no-shows at the clinic. Unlike a patient who cancels their own appointment in advance, a caregiver conflict often appears as a no-show with no warning.

Patient Severity and Scheduling

The simulation tool assigns each patient a severity score from 1 to 10, which is a composite measure of cognitive impairment. Lower scores represent patients in early stages of decline; higher scores represent patients at more advanced stages who may have significant memory loss, behavioral changes, and limited ability to live independently. Severity affects scheduling in three important ways:

Visit Duration

Expected visit durations range from approximately 50 minutes for mild cases to over 110 minutes for the most complex. When a schedule assigns the same slot length to all patients regardless of severity, mild patients finish early (wasted time) and severe patients run over (causing cascading delays).

No-Show Risk

Patients with higher severity scores are more likely to miss appointments. Advanced dementia affects daily routines, increases dependence on caregivers, and makes the logistics of an out-of-home appointment more challenging. The simulation reflects this pattern.

Provider Assignment

The clinic has a policy of reserving certain providers for higher-severity patients. Some providers see only the most severe cases, while others see patients with lower severity scores. The simulation allows you to activate or deactivate this routing and observe its operational effects.

Data & Schedule

The various data sources and types available in the case study are introduced below.

The Provider Schedule

The clinic has four providers (their pseudonyms were chosen for buildings on ODU’s campus): Dr. Rollins; Dr. Constant; Dr. Kaufman; and Dr. Gornto. Each works a fixed weekly schedule that does not change across the year. Their combined schedule produces 10 provider-shifts per weekday (across all four providers working AM and PM blocks), for a total of approximately 22 individual provider-shifts per week.

Each shift is a five-hour block. The simulation models patient arrivals within that window, including late arrivals, the time the provider spends with each patient, buffer time (if any) between appointments, and any overrun that spills past the end of the shift.

Provider schedules are treated as fixed in this simulation. You cannot reassign which providers work which days because that is a constraint of the clinic’s operational reality. Physician schedules at Goldrich include patient care, administrative responsibilities, teaching, and research. Your job is to find the best policies within the constraint of their existing schedule.

As an example, Friday afternoon shifts are staffed by a single provider. Therefore, if a single patient fails to appear for an appointment on a Friday afternoon, the provider spends a meaningful fraction of that shift idle, instead of seeing patients.

Weather Data

The simulation incorporates weather to reflect the reality of patient arrivals. Rain and snow increase the probability that patients arrive late, with an additional penalty for patients who are driving long distances. A patient driving from the Eastern Shore on a snowy morning faces a meaningfully different journey than a patient driving five minutes from downtown Norfolk. The weather data in the simulation is real: the data comes from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration’s weather station at the Norfolk Airport.

Patient Data

The simulation uses a synthetic patient population derived from US Census data, not actual patient records from the Goldrich Institute. Patient health information is protected under federal law (HIPAA) and under the ethical principles of medical research.

Instead, the simulation draws from a population of 3,120 synthetic patients generated using American Community Survey (ACS) Census data for the clinic’s 30-ZIP-code catchment area. Each patient is assigned a home ZIP code (weighted by the 55+ population in that area), a drive time to the clinic, a severity score drawn from a realistic distribution, and an expected visit duration based on their severity. The resulting population is statistically representative of the kinds of patients the clinic serves.

Utilization Rate

The CMC currently has a utilization rate around 60%, which is reflected in the Baseline policy in the simulation tool. Improving utilization rate generally benefits both patients (more of whom can be seen by providers) and providers (idle time waiting for patients is time that might have been better spent otherwise). For the clinic, a relatively low utilization rate may indicate lost revenue. The goal of our case is not to overburden the providers, nor to hurry patients who are being seen. Instead, our goal is to improve decision-making at the CMC to better match existing capacity and resources to the needs of patients.

KPIs & Visualizations

The simulation tool provides outcomes for a scheduling policy of your choice, and compares your policy against the baseline using several Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) and figures.

Key Performance Indicators

Utilization Rate

The fill rate of available appointment slots, with baseline policy around 60%.

Utilization rate = (Slots in which a patient arrived and was treated) ÷ (All available slots) × 100

Total Patients Seen

The count of all patients seen by all providers across all shifts.

Average Idle Time per Shift

In each shift of 300 minutes, the amount of time in which the provider was not engaged in patient visits.

Shifts Running Over

The proportion of shifts where the last patient’s appointment extended past the end of the shift window.

Total No-Shows

The count of patients who were scheduled but did not attend the appointment and failed to cancel in advance.

Average Patient Wait

The average time patients spent waiting between their scheduled appointment and the actual time at which they were seen by the provider.

Provider Equity Index

A measure of fairness for the distribution of workload among providers.

Empty Shifts

The count of provider shifts in which 0 patients were seen.

Adjustable Settings

Select each tab for more information about the policy levers available in Block 2.

Capacity decisions control how many patients are scheduled per shift and how much time is allocated to each appointment. These are the most direct levers for improving utilization.

  • Patients per Shift — The number of appointment slots scheduled per provider per shift. At 75 min/slot in a 300-min shift, 4 is the realistic maximum. Reducing this raises the fill rate but reduces throughput. Range: 2–4 patients.
  • Slot Duration (minutes) — The length of each appointment block. Applies only when severity-tiered slots are off. Longer slots reduce overrun risk but reduce capacity. Range: 45–100 minutes.

Severity rules shape how patient complexity is distributed across shifts. Because high-severity patients require significantly longer visits, concentrating too many in a single shift compounds overrun risk and cascade delays.

  • Maximum High-Severity Patients per Shift — Caps the number of severity 7–10 patients in a single shift. Prevents multiple long, complex visits from stacking. Trade-off: high-severity patients may face longer waits. Range: 0–4.

Resilience settings introduce slack into the schedule to absorb disruptions before they cascade to subsequent patients. The trade-off is always reduced capacity.

  • Buffer Time Between Appointments — Inserts a scheduled gap for documentation and room turnover. A 10-minute buffer across 4 slots costs 40 minutes of capacity per shift. Range: 0–20 minutes.

Advanced policies allow more targeted interventions in how patients are matched to slots and how demand is hedged. Each involves meaningful trade-offs.

  • Severity-Tiered Slot Lengths — Low (1–3) = 60 min, Moderate (4–6) = 75 min, High (7–10) = 95 min. Reduces both idle time and overrun risk. Range: On/Off.
  • Cluster High-Severity Patients on One Day — Routes all severity 7–10 patients to a single designated weekday, leaving all other days lighter. Range: Mon/Tue/Wed/Thu/Fri/Off.
  • Overbook by 1 Patient per Shift — Hedges against the ~10% no-show rate. Raises throughput on average but causes overruns when all patients show up. Range: On/Off.
  • Geographic Scheduling Hedge — Books patients driving 40+ minutes in middle slots rather than first or last, reducing cascade risk from late-arriving distant patients. Range: On/Off.

These settings correspond to the extra credit question in the assignment and reflect two real structural policies currently in place at the CMC.

  • Rotate Undesirable Shifts Equitably — Distributes patient load evenly across all four providers, reducing the Provider Equity Index. No direct effect on utilization or throughput. Range: On/Off.
  • Severity-Based Provider Routing — Routes high-severity patients (7–10) exclusively to Dr. Rollins and Dr. Constant; low-to-moderate severity patients exclusively to Dr. Kaufman and Dr. Gornto. The current CMC policy is On. Range: On/Off.

You will use the simulation tool to diagnose the clinic’s current scheduling inefficiencies, design and test one or more scheduling policies, and deliver an evidence-based recommendation to clinic leadership.

Your Task

Begin by running Block 1 of the simulation notebook to load the baseline. Examine the utilization heatmap and KPI scorecard before you change anything. Your goal in this step is to build a clear picture of where the inefficiency is concentrated.

  • Which shifts have the lowest utilization?
  • Which providers carry the heaviest load?
  • How much idle time is the clinic experiencing per shift on average?
  • What percentage of shifts run over their scheduled window?
  • Are there patterns by day of week, time of day, or season that stand out?

Identify two or three specific, evidence-backed causes of the gap. Use the figures from the simulation tool (heatmap, day-of-week chart, idle time by provider) to support your argument.

Using Block 2 of the simulation, design a scheduling policy by adjusting one or more of the available levers. Run your policy against the baseline and examine the results in Block 3.

Your presentation must walk through each non-default setting you activated, organized clearly. For each policy choice, explain:

  • What you changed?
  • Why you made this choice?
  • What you expected to happen?
  • What actually happened?

Where relevant, show how your choices interact. The logic connecting your decisions should be explicit, not implied. You are encouraged to run the simulation multiple times with different settings before settling on a final policy.

Your presentation must include visualizations to support your argument. The simulation tool exports a set of figures automatically each time you run Block 3. I encourage you to use these figures.

You are welcome to create your own figures if you believe they communicate something more effectively. This is not required, but it is recognized in the rubric.

All figures should be labeled, referenced explicitly in your narration, and connected to a specific conclusion. Figures that appear on screen without commentary will not receive credit.

Close your presentation with a clear, specific, and actionable recommendation for the leadership of the Goldrich Institute. Your audience is a senior healthcare administrator. Your recommendation should address:

  • Exactly which scheduling changes you recommend, and what you expect them to achieve.
  • The most significant trade-off your policy involves, and why it is an acceptable one given the clinic’s goals.
  • One limitation of your analysis — what assumption would you want to validate with real clinic data before fully committing to this recommendation.

The Goldrich Institute currently operates under two structural policies that are treated as fixed constraints in the simulation. For one extra credit point, evaluate these policies from a managerial perspective.

Annual schedule setting. Provider schedules are fixed for the entire calendar year. Providers know their shifts well in advance, but the schedule cannot adapt to changes in demand, patient mix, or staffing disruptions during the year.

Severity-based provider routing. High-severity patients (scores 7–10) are routed exclusively to Dr. Rollins and Dr. Constant. Low-to-moderate severity patients are routed exclusively to Dr. Kaufman and Dr. Gornto.

Your analysis should address:

  • What operational and scheduling benefits do these policies provide?
  • What costs or risks do they introduce for patients, for providers, and for the clinic as an organization?
  • Would you recommend modifying either policy? If so, how, and what would you need to know before bringing that recommendation to leadership?
